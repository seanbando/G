# Voice-Over Script: Follow $1 From Your Pocket to Wall Street: The Stock Market Explained

Recording sheet for the whiteboard video. Every spoken line is numbered (VO-01, VO-02 …) so audio files can be named to match and dropped onto the timeline in order.

**All numbers, dollar amounts, dates, times and abbreviations are written out in words**, so read them exactly as written.

## Cast: 4 voices

| # | Voice | Lines | Words | Character direction | Role in the story |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **NARRATOR** | 30 | 1649 | Main host. Warm, clear, conversational, like explaining money to a friend. Steady pace, about 160 words per minute. | The whole story |
| 2 | **YOU** | 3 | 15 | The viewer's inner voice. Curious and a little surprised. Younger and more casual than the narrator. Must sound clearly different from the narrator. | Asks the questions the audience is thinking |
| 3 | **THE APP** | 1 | 14 | The investing app, personified. Upbeat, efficient, slightly robotic customer-service tone. | Confirms the order |
| 4 | **THE EXCHANGE** | 1 | 17 | The stock exchange, personified. Calm, official, matter-of-fact, like an announcer or matchmaker. | Matches buyer and seller |

**Total:** 35 lines · 1695 spoken words · about 10.6 minutes of audio at 160 wpm, before pauses.

**People who are drawn but don't speak:** Maya, Chris and Jordan (the stock-sale example) and the retired teacher in Arizona. The narrator describes them, so draw them but don't cast voices for them.

### Recording tips
- **One voice actor can do it.** The narrator can also voice THE APP and THE EXCHANGE with a filter (a phone-speaker effect for THE APP, a slight echo for THE EXCHANGE). **YOU** should be a different person or a clearly different AI voice so viewers never mix it up with the narrator.
- **Name each file after its line ID,** for example `VO-02_YOU.wav`, so the editor can line the audio up in order.
- **Leave about half a second of silence** at the start and end of each file. Drawing usually takes longer than speaking, so the editor will add gaps.
- **Read every line as a full sentence.** Don't clip character lines into short fragments.
- **Letter-by-letter names:** "D-T-C-C" and "S-I-P-C" are written with hyphens so they're read one letter at a time, and "U.S." is read "you-ess".

## Part 1: Full script in order

Read this top to bottom for timing and context. 🎨 marks a drawing cue for the animator; it isn't read aloud.

**VO-01 · NARRATOR**  
When you tap "buy" on a stock, your order can be out of your phone and on a trading firm's computers in less than a second. Some orders end up on systems in New Jersey, not Wall Street. And the company whose name is on the button usually doesn't get a single cent of your money.

**VO-02 · YOU**  
Wait, so who does?

**VO-03 · NARRATOR**  
That's what we're going to find out. We're going to follow one dollar from your pocket to the stranger who ends up with it, and by the end you'll know what you bought, who touched your order, and how people actually make money from it.

**VO-04 · NARRATOR**  
First, two quick definitions. The stock market is a place where buyers and sellers meet to trade shares of public companies like Apple, Meta, and Alphabet. A stock is a piece of ownership in one of those companies.

**VO-05 · NARRATOR**  
Now let me ask you a question. Company A's stock costs five hundred dollars a share. Company B's stock costs five dollars a share. Which company is worth more?

**VO-06 · YOU**  
Company A, obviously.

**VO-07 · NARRATOR**  
That was a trap, because I didn't give you enough information.

> 🎨 *Draw: Two pizzas side by side. Pizza A cut into 3 slices labelled $500. Pizza B cut into a million tiny slices labelled $5.*

**VO-08 · NARRATOR**  
Think of each company as a pizza and each share as a slice. Company A is a pizza cut into only three slices, and each slice costs five hundred dollars, so the whole company is worth fifteen hundred dollars. Company B is cut into a million slices at five dollars each, so it's worth five million dollars. The cheap stock belongs to the company that's worth over three thousand times more.

**VO-09 · NARRATOR**  
The price of one share tells you almost nothing on its own. What matters is the price multiplied by the number of shares, which is called market value. Apple has roughly fifteen billion shares, which is why a few hundred dollars a share adds up to trillions. So when something sounds too simple, it's worth asking a follow-up question.

**VO-10 · NARRATOR**  
Now let's spend your dollar. Say you open an investing app on a Tuesday morning and buy one dollar of Apple. A whole share costs a few hundred dollars, so your dollar buys a small fraction of one share. It's tiny, but it's real ownership.

**VO-11 · THE APP**  
Your order is in. I'm buying one dollar of Apple at the market price.

**VO-12 · NARRATOR**  
A market order tells your broker to buy right away at the best price available. The price on your screen isn't guaranteed, because it can move while your order is being filled. Your broker may also group your fractional order with other customers' orders before it buys.

**VO-13 · NARRATOR**  
Many retail orders don't go straight to an exchange. The broker often sends them to a market maker, which is a trading firm like Citadel Securities or Virtu that's always ready to buy and sell. It works a bit like a currency booth at the airport. It quotes one price to buy and a slightly higher price to sell, and the gap between them is called the spread. If it will buy Apple at two hundred fifty dollars even and sell at two hundred fifty dollars and two cents, that's a two-cent spread on a whole share. That isn't guaranteed profit, because the stock can move against the market maker a minute later. Citadel Securities says it handles about a third of all U.S. retail stock trading volume.

**VO-14 · NARRATOR**  
Market makers often pay brokers for sending them orders, which is called payment for order flow, and it's one reason many apps charge zero commission. Critics say it gives brokers a reason to route orders where they're paid most. Brokers are still required to seek the best execution reasonably available for your order.

**VO-15 · NARRATOR**  
The market maker might sell to you from its own inventory, or it might find shares somewhere else, such as a stock exchange.

> 🎨 *Draw: Map of New Jersey with two dots, Mahwah and Carteret*

**VO-16 · NARRATOR**  
The New York Stock Exchange still rings its opening bell at nine thirty in the morning, Eastern time, from its floor at eleven Wall Street. But its trading systems run in a data center in Mahwah, New Jersey, and Nasdaq's main U.S. data center is in Carteret, New Jersey. The NYSE traces its roots to seventeen ninety-two, when twenty-four brokers signed an agreement in Lower Manhattan.

**VO-17 · NARRATOR**  
Regular trading runs from nine thirty in the morning to four in the afternoon, Eastern time, on weekdays. Some brokers also let you trade in the premarket session, which can start as early as four in the morning, and in after-hours trading until eight at night. Fewer people trade at those times, so prices can jump more and the gap between buying and selling prices is often wider.

**VO-18 · THE EXCHANGE**  
I have a buyer and a seller at the same price, so you two are a match.

**VO-19 · NARRATOR**  
So who got your dollar? It went to whoever sold you that sliver. That might be the market maker itself, a retired teacher in Arizona selling some stock to pay for a new roof, or an institution like a pension fund, a mutual fund, or a hedge fund. Retail investors like you are one group in the market, and big institutions are another. The companies themselves can move their own stock too, when they report earnings, buy back shares, or sell new ones.

**VO-20 · YOU**  
So Apple doesn't get my dollar at all?

**VO-21 · NARRATOR**  
Not from this trade, it doesn't. A company raises money when it sells new shares, like in its initial public offering. Apple went public on December twelfth, nineteen eighty, at twenty-two dollars a share and raised roughly a hundred million dollars. Almost every Apple trade since then has been between investors, like buying a concert ticket from another fan after the box office has sold out.

**VO-22 · NARRATOR**  
Your trade still has to become official. Since May of twenty twenty-four, most U.S. stock trades settle one business day later, so your Tuesday trade usually becomes official on Wednesday. A company called the D-T-C-C stands in the middle, guarantees eligible trades, and settles what each firm owes. On the official records, most American shares are registered to a company called Cede and Company, but you're the beneficial owner, so the gains and the losses are yours.

**VO-23 · NARRATOR**  
If your broker is a member of S-I-P-C, the Securities Investor Protection Corporation, and it fails with customer assets missing, it generally protects up to five hundred thousand dollars per customer, including up to two hundred fifty thousand dollars in cash. It doesn't protect you if the stock simply falls.

**VO-24 · NARRATOR**  
So how do you actually make money from owning a stock? There are two main ways.

**VO-25 · NARRATOR**  
The first is selling for more than you paid. Say Maya buys ten shares of a company at two hundred dollars each from a seller named Chris. A year later, the company is doing well, and Jordan wants those shares and is willing to pay two hundred fifty dollars a share. Maya sells, and she's made five hundred dollars before taxes. If Jordan had only been willing to pay one hundred fifty dollars a share, she'd be down five hundred dollars instead. Maya never needed to meet Chris or Jordan, because the market found them for her.

> 🎨 *Draw: Three stick figures in a row. Shares pass from Chris to Maya to Jordan; money passes the other way.*

**VO-26 · NARRATOR**  
The second way is dividends, which are part of a company's profits paid out to shareholders. Not every company pays them, and they can be cut.

**VO-27 · NARRATOR**  
So why does the price change at all? Every trade has a buyer and a seller. The price rises when buyers are willing to pay more, and it falls when sellers are willing to accept less.

**VO-28 · NARRATOR**  
Imagine Apple's last trade was at two hundred fifty dollars even, and the lowest anyone will sell for right now is two hundred fifty dollars and five cents. A buyer who really wants in pays it, and the trade goes through. The last price is now two hundred fifty dollars and five cents, and that's the number on your screen. Thousands of small decisions like that, every second, are what make a stock chart move.

**VO-29 · NARRATOR**  
What changes their minds is mostly what they expect the company to earn. A strong earnings report or a lawsuit can shift those expectations in minutes, and so can fear about the whole economy.

**VO-30 · NARRATOR**  
In his nineteen eighty-seven letter to shareholders, Warren Buffett passed on a lesson from his teacher, Benjamin Graham: in the short run, the market is a voting machine, but in the long run it's a weighing machine. Day to day, prices follow moods. Over many years, they tend to follow profits.

**VO-31 · NARRATOR**  
Now let's change one thing. Instead of buying Apple, you put your dollar into an S and P five hundred index fund, which is one common way to own a basket of companies. You buy a slice of the fund, and the fund owns stock in roughly five hundred large American companies, with the biggest companies getting the biggest weight. According to Gallup, sixty-two percent of U.S. adults said they owned stock in twenty twenty-five, whether directly, through a fund, or in a retirement account.

**VO-32 · NARRATOR**  
Historically, over long periods, U.S. large-company stocks have returned roughly ten percent a year on average, including dividends. As an illustration, not a forecast, a dollar growing at a steady ten percent a year would become about forty-five dollars after forty years, before inflation, fees, and taxes.

**VO-33 · NARRATOR**  
Real markets don't move in a straight line. From late two thousand seven to early two thousand nine, the S and P five hundred lost more than half its value. In twenty twenty, it fell almost thirty-four percent between February nineteenth and March twenty-third. The broad index recovered both times, but individual stocks don't always come back.

**VO-34 · NARRATOR**  
So here's the whole trip. Your dollar bought a slice of ownership. Your order went from your broker to a market maker or an exchange, and was matched with a seller who wanted out. The next business day, the system made it official, and your app recorded the sliver as yours. The company on the button didn't get your dollar, but you now own a tiny piece of it.

**VO-35 · NARRATOR**  
So here's my question for you. Before this video, would you have picked the five-hundred-dollar stock as the bigger company? Tell me in the comments, and subscribe if you want more of the money system explained one dollar at a time.
