Follow $1 From Your Pocket to Wall Street: The Stock Market Explained

[DRAW: A hand taps a phone screen with a big "BUY" button that says "Apple." A dollar bill with a face (our hero, "the dollar") flies out of the phone. Apple's logo watches with an empty hand.]

When you tap "buy" on a stock, your money almost never goes to the company whose name is on the button. If you buy one dollar of Apple, Apple usually doesn't get a single cent of it.

[DRAW: YOU, a stick figure holding a phone, with a big question mark over their head.]

YOU: Wait, then who gets it?

[DRAW: A question mark next to the dollar. Then an official-looking paper with "OWNER: ???" written on it.]

That's what we're going to find out. And here's something even stranger. On the official paperwork, the stock you buy usually isn't in your name.

[DRAW: The "OWNER: ???" paper with a magnifying glass over it. Write "Answer at Stop 5."]

By the end of this video, you'll know whose name is on it, and why that's totally fine.

[DRAW: The road map, drawn left to right: 1 Your App, 2 Market Maker, 3 Exchange, 4 Seller, 5 Clearinghouse. The dollar stands at the start. This map returns between every stop.]

We're going to follow one dollar through five stops. At every stop, I'll tell you who's there and what they do, in plain English.

[DRAW: The dollar holding a shopping bag with a question mark on it.]

But first, you need to know what your dollar is buying.

[DRAW: A company building split into puzzle pieces. One piece is labelled "1 share = a piece of ownership." Then a crowd trading pieces, labelled "Stock market."]

A stock is a small piece of ownership in a company. The stock market is simply the place where people buy and sell those pieces.

[DRAW: Two price tags. "Company A: $500 a share." "Company B: $5 a share." A big question mark between them.]

So here's a quick test. Company A's stock costs five hundred dollars a share. Company B's stock costs five dollars a share. Which company is worth more?

[PAUSE: 3 seconds. On screen: "Pause and guess: A or B?"]

[DRAW: YOU points confidently at Company A.]

YOU: Company A, obviously.

[DRAW: A trap door opens under the "Company A" price tag.]

That was a trap, because I didn't give you enough information.

[DRAW: Pizza A cut into 3 slices, each labelled $500. Write "3 × $500 = $1,500" under it.]

Think of each company as a pizza and each share as a slice. Company A is cut into only three slices, and each slice costs five hundred dollars, so the whole pizza is worth fifteen hundred dollars.

[DRAW: Pizza B cut into a huge number of tiny slices, labelled $5. Write "1,000,000 × $5 = $5,000,000" under it. Pizza B is drawn much bigger.]

Company B is cut into a million slices at five dollars each, so the whole pizza is worth five million dollars. The cheap slice came from the much bigger pizza.

[DRAW: Write "Price of one share × number of shares = size of the company."]

So the price of one share doesn't tell you how big a company is. You have to multiply it by the number of shares.

[DRAW: A hand slips a note into a pocket. The note says "Ask one more question."]

Keep that trick in your pocket, because a lot of things in the stock market sound simple until you ask one more question.

[DRAW: The road map. A circle around Stop 1, Your App. The dollar walks to it.]

Now let's spend your dollar. Stop one is your app.

[DRAW: A calendar page that says "Tuesday." The phone shows "Buy $1 of Apple."]

Say it's Tuesday morning, and you open an investing app and buy one dollar of Apple.

[DRAW: A whole pizza labelled "1 Apple share = a few hundred dollars," with one bite-sized piece cut out and labelled "your $1."]

One Apple share costs a few hundred dollars, so your dollar buys a tiny slice of one share, kind of like buying one bite of a whole pizza.

[DRAW: THE APP, a phone with a friendly face, giving a thumbs up.]

THE APP: Got it. I'm buying one dollar of Apple at the market price.

[DRAW: YOU tilts their head at the phone.]

YOU: What's the market price?

[DRAW: THE APP points to a price tag that wobbles slightly: "$250.00 … $250.03."]

THE APP: It's the best price I can get for you right now. It might move a few cents before your order goes through.

[DRAW: The dollar turns into a paper slip labelled "ORDER: buy $1 of Apple." A small sign reads "Looking for a seller…"]

That's all that happens at stop one. Your dollar has turned into an order, and now the app needs to find someone who will sell you that slice.

[DRAW: The road map. A check mark on Stop 1. The order slip walks to Stop 2, Market Maker.]

Stop two is the market maker.

[DRAW: YOU with a confused face and three question marks.]

YOU: The market what?

[DRAW: A big building with a trading screen, labelled "Market maker = big trading company." An arrow turns it into a corner store with shelves of Apple stock.]

A market maker is a big trading company that's always ready to buy or sell. Think of it as a store that always keeps Apple stock on the shelf.

[DRAW: The store's sign shows two prices: "We buy: lower. We sell: higher."]

Like any store, it buys a little cheaper than it sells.

[DRAW: THE MARKET MAKER, a friendly clerk behind the counter, pointing at two signs: "We buy at $250.00" and "We sell at $250.02."]

THE MARKET MAKER: I'll buy Apple from you at two hundred fifty dollars, or sell it to you at two hundred fifty dollars and two cents. I'm open all day.

[DRAW: Zoom in on the gap between the two signs. Write "2¢." A small coin goes into the clerk's jar, then a small coin falls out.]

That tiny two-cent gap is how the market maker tries to make money, although it can lose money too if the price moves the wrong way.

[DRAW: Two store signs: "Citadel Securities" and "Virtu."]

Two of the biggest market makers are Citadel Securities and Virtu.

[DRAW: The clerk hands THE APP a small envelope. THE APP shows a sign that says "$0 trades."]

Here's something most people don't know. Market makers often pay your app to send them your orders. That's one reason many apps can let you trade for free.

[DRAW: THE APP holding a magnifying glass over price tags, looking for the best one.]

Your app is still required to look for a good price for you.

[DRAW: Two arrows from the store. One arrow: "Has it: sold off the shelf." The other arrow: "Doesn't have it: go to Stop 3."]

If the market maker has the stock, it can sell it to you straight off the shelf. If it doesn't, your order can head to stop three.

[DRAW: The road map. A check mark on Stop 2. The order slip walks to Stop 3, Exchange.]

Stop three is the stock exchange. This is what most people picture when they hear "Wall Street."

[DRAW: A street sign that says "Wall St." with a big bell ringing. Write "9:30 a.m."]

The New York Stock Exchange still rings its famous opening bell on Wall Street at nine thirty in the morning, Eastern time.

[DRAW: An arrow from the bell across a river to a plain, boxy building labelled "Data center, New Jersey," full of blinking computers.]

But here's the twist. The computers that actually match the trades sit in data centers in New Jersey.

[DRAW: THE EXCHANGE, a building with a face, holding two lists: "Want to buy" and "Want to sell."]

An exchange works like a matchmaker. It keeps one list of people who want to buy and another list of people who want to sell.

[DRAW: A line drawn from a name on the "buy" list to a name on the "sell" list, with a heart in the middle.]

When a buyer and a seller agree on a price, it puts them together.

[DRAW: THE EXCHANGE joining the buyer's and seller's hands.]

THE EXCHANGE: I have a buyer and a seller at the same price, so you two are a match.

[DRAW: A clock with the hours from 9:30 to 4:00 shaded in. A tiny "Mon to Fri" calendar.]

The market is open from nine thirty in the morning until four in the afternoon, Eastern time, on weekdays.

[DRAW: The shaded clock with small zigzag lines drawn before 9:30 and after 4:00.]

Some apps let you trade earlier or later, but fewer people are trading then, so prices can jump around more.

[DRAW: The road map. A check mark on Stop 3. A big question mark over Stop 4.]

So your order has found a match. That means it's time to answer the very first question. Who got your dollar? Take a guess.

[PAUSE: 3 seconds. On screen: "A) Apple  B) Your app  C) A stranger"]

[DRAW: Circle "C) A stranger." The dollar walks into the hands of a group: a store clerk, a big fund building, and a retired teacher.]

It's the stranger, and that's stop four. Your dollar goes to whoever sold you that slice. That could be the market maker itself, a big investment fund, or a regular person, like a retired teacher in Arizona.

[DRAW: THE SELLER, a retired teacher with glasses, holding a "New roof" estimate and waving the dollar.]

THE SELLER: I'm selling a little Apple to help pay for a new roof. Thanks for the dollar.

[DRAW: YOU looking at the Apple logo, which shrugs with empty pockets.]

YOU: So Apple gets nothing?

[DRAW: A calendar page that says "December 1980." Apple's logo holds a bag of money labelled "First sale of shares."]

Not from your trade, it doesn't. Apple got money from its stock when it first sold shares to the public, back in December of nineteen eighty.

[DRAW: Apple shares being passed hand to hand along a long line of people, while Apple's logo watches from the side.]

Since then, almost every Apple share has simply been passed from one investor to another.

[DRAW: A box office sells a concert ticket to Fan 1. Money goes to the band.]

It works like a concert ticket. The band gets paid when the ticket is first sold.

[DRAW: Fan 1 sells the same ticket to you. The money goes to Fan 1, and the band shrugs.]

If you buy that ticket later from another fan, your money goes to the fan, not the band.

[DRAW: The road map. A check mark on Stop 4. The order slip walks to Stop 5, Clearinghouse.]

Stop five is where your trade becomes official. Your trade is matched, but the paperwork isn't finished yet.

[DRAW: Two calendar pages: "Tuesday: trade" with an arrow to "Wednesday: final," with a stamp that says "OFFICIAL."]

Most trades become final one business day later, so your Tuesday trade is final on Wednesday.

[DRAW: THE CLEARINGHOUSE, a referee with a whistle standing between the buyer and the seller.]

THE CLEARINGHOUSE: Nobody walks away until everyone gets paid.

[DRAW: The referee holds a sign that says "D-T-C-C." Money moves one way and the stock moves the other, with the referee in the middle.]

That's the clearinghouse, a company called the D-T-C-C. It sits in the middle of the trade and makes sure the money and the stock end up where they're supposed to, even if one side can't pay.

[DRAW: The "OWNER: ???" paper from the opening comes back. The question marks are erased and replaced with "Cede & Co."]

And now, the paperwork I promised you at the start. On the official records, most American stock is registered to a company called Cede and Company.

[DRAW: YOU looks shocked, holding the paper.]

YOU: Hold on. So it's not my stock?

[DRAW: A coat check counter at a restaurant. A coat hangs on a hook.]

It is your stock. Think of a coat check at a restaurant. The coat check holds your coat and keeps track of the ticket, but the coat still belongs to you.

[DRAW: The coat's tag says "Cede & Co." The ticket in YOU's hand says "Yours." THE APP holds a notebook that says "Slice of Apple: belongs to YOU."]

Cede and Company holds the paperwork so trades can move fast, and your app keeps the record that says the slice is yours.

[DRAW: An Apple price arrow going up, with YOU smiling, and an arrow going down, with YOU frowning.]

If Apple goes up, you gain, and if it goes down, you lose.

[DRAW: YOU looking worried at THE APP, which has a "Closed" sign on it.]

YOU: Okay, but what if my app goes out of business?

[DRAW: A shield labelled "S-I-P-C: up to $500,000" standing in front of YOU's account, while THE APP's building crumbles behind it.]

If the company behind your app is a member of S-I-P-C, your account is protected up to five hundred thousand dollars if the company fails and your investments go missing.

[DRAW: A falling stock arrow passes straight past the shield, labelled "Not covered."]

It doesn't protect you if the stock simply goes down.

[DRAW: The road map with all five stops checked off. The dollar's path is drawn as a dotted line.]

So here's where we are. Your dollar became an order in your app, went to a market maker or an exchange, landed with a stranger who wanted to sell, and became official the next day.

[DRAW: A stopwatch reading "less than 1 second."]

That's five stops, and most of it happened in less than a second.

[DRAW: The dollar looks at itself in a mirror, and the reflection shows a stack of dollars.]

But you probably didn't come here just to find out where your dollar goes. You want to know how it turns into more dollars.

[DRAW: Write "2 ways to make money." Number 1: "Sell for more than you paid." Number 2 is left blank for now.]

There are two main ways to make money from a stock. The first is selling it for more than you paid. Let's meet Maya.

[DRAW: A calendar flips forward one year. A company chart arrow goes up. JORDAN walks in.]

She made five hundred dollars before taxes. But if Jordan had only offered one hundred fifty dollars a share, she'd have lost five hundred dollars instead. Selling for more is never guaranteed.

[DRAW: Fill in Number 2: "Dividends." A company hands a small slice of a money pie to a shareholder.]

The second way to make money is dividends. Some companies share part of their profits with the people who own their stock, usually a few times a year. Not every company does it, and they can stop.

Because the price of a stock is just the last price someone agreed to pay. If buyers are willing to pay more, the price goes up. If sellers are willing to take less, the price goes down.

It's two hundred fifty dollars and five cents, because the price you see is simply the last trade.

[DRAW: Many tiny handshakes forming a zigzag stock chart line.]

Millions of little deals like that, every day, are what make a stock chart go up and down.

[DRAW: A thought bubble over a crowd of buyers showing a company with a money bag labelled "future profits."]

What makes people willing to pay more or less? Mostly, it's what they think the company will earn in the future.

[DRAW: A newspaper reading "Good news" with the price arrow going up, and a newspaper reading "Bad news" with the arrow going down.]
