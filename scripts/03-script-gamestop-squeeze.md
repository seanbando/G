# "I Like the Stock" — How a Mall Game Shop Broke Wall Street

**Format:** Story-driven whiteboard explainer · ~2,500 spoken words · ~15–16 min at ~160 wpm
**Voices:** Narrator + character voices (bold role names). Each character = a drawn figure with a speech bubble.
**Callback line:** "I like the stock."
**Running example character:** Sam and the sneakers (explains short selling)

> On-screen note at 0:05: *Not financial advice. For education only.*

---

## Chapter 1: Four Words

On the twenty-seventh of January, twenty twenty-one, a video game shop from the mall was one of the hottest stocks on Earth.
[DRAW: A small mall storefront labelled "GameStop". A rocket bursting out of its roof.]

Its stock had gone up more than seventeen hundred percent in four weeks.

No new console. No big hit game. No surprise profits.

**TRADER 1:** "What did they sell?"
**TRADER 2:** "Nothing new."
**TRADER 1:** "Then who's buying?"
**TRADER 2:** "...Everyone?"

One hedge fund lost more than half its money in a month.
A trading app stopped people from buying.
Congress called a hearing.
[DRAW: Three quick panels: a falling money bag, a phone with a red "BUY" button crossed out, the Capitol dome.]

And the man at the centre of it all explained everything in four words.

**KEITH GILL:** "I like the stock."

So how did a shop selling used video games break Wall Street?

The short answer is: a bet against the shop, a man in a headband, and millions of people on their phones.

The long answer starts two years earlier, in a basement in Massachusetts.

## Chapter 2: The Cat in the Headband

Keith Gill wasn't a hedge fund manager.
[DRAW: Ordinary guy at a desk. Red headband. A poster of a kitten on the wall.]

He worked in financial education at an insurance company. In his spare time, he made YouTube videos as "Roaring Kitty."

In twenty nineteen, he looked at GameStop and saw something most of Wall Street didn't.

**WALL STREET:** "Malls are dying. Games are going digital. GameStop is Blockbuster with controllers."
**KEITH GILL:** "Maybe. But it's cheaper than it should be."

So he bet on it. About fifty-three thousand dollars.
That's roughly the price of a new pickup truck, on one struggling store.
[DRAW: A pickup truck parked on a pile of game cases.]

He posted his bet on Reddit, on a forum called WallStreetBets.
Picture a giant group chat where people share their wildest trades, and roast each other for the losers.

**REDDIT:** "Lol."
**REDDIT:** "Bro."
**REDDIT:** "See you at zero."

Every month or so, he posted an update. A screenshot of his account. Up. Down. Up again.

He didn't sell.

**REDDIT:** "Why are you still holding?"
**KEITH GILL:** "I like the stock."

By the spring of twenty twenty, during the pandemic, GameStop's stock fell under three dollars.
That's less than a used game in its own bargain bin.
[DRAW: A share certificate in the "$2.99" bargain bin.]

But some very rich people had made the opposite bet. To understand what happened next, you need to know how you bet *against* a company.

## Chapter 3: Borrowing Sneakers

It's called short selling. And it's easier to see with sneakers.
[DRAW: Two stick figures. One holds a shoebox labelled "LIMITED".]

Your friend Sam owns a pair of limited-edition sneakers worth two hundred dollars.

You think the hype is about to die. So you borrow Sam's sneakers.

**SAM:** "You'll give them back?"
**YOU:** "Promise."

Then you sell them to a stranger for two hundred dollars.
[DRAW: You hand the box to a stranger; $200 floats back.]

Next month, the hype dies. The same sneakers now sell for fifty.
You buy a pair for fifty, give them back to Sam, and keep a hundred and fifty dollars.

That's shorting. Sell first. Buy back later. Hope the price drops.

But here's the danger.
What if the price goes *up*?

The sneakers jump to five hundred. Then a thousand.
**SAM:** "I'd like my sneakers back now."
**YOU:** "...How about Tuesday?"
**SAM:** "Now."

You have to buy them back, at any price. And there's no limit on how high that price can go.
[DRAW: A price tag that keeps growing until it runs off the whiteboard.]

Now imagine *lots* of people borrowed sneakers from lots of Sams. And they all have to buy them back at the same time.
Everyone buying pushes the price up. A higher price forces even more people to buy.
That's a short squeeze.
[DRAW: A spiral arrow: "price up → forced buying → price up".]

Hold that thought.

In January twenty twenty-one, investors had bet against GameStop so hard that the number of shares sold short was *bigger than the number of shares available to trade.* Reports put it around a hundred and forty percent.

**SAM:** "Wait. How did you borrow more sneakers than exist?"

Because a share can be borrowed, sold, and then lent out again by its new owner. Like one pair of sneakers that ends up promised to three different people.
[DRAW: One shoebox with three hands reaching for it.]

One of the biggest bets against GameStop belonged to a hedge fund called Melvin Capital.

## Chapter 4: The Spark

**MELVIN:** "Dying malls. Dying business. Easy money."
[DRAW: A suit in a glass tower, feet on the desk, pointing down at a tiny GameStop.]

But by early twenty twenty-one, two things had changed.

First, a billionaire showed up.
Ryan Cohen, who co-founded the pet supply site Chewy, had bought a big chunk of GameStop. In January, he got seats on its board.

His pitch, roughly: turn GameStop from a mall shop into an online gaming giant.
**MELVIN:** "Sure."

The stock started to climb.

Second, Reddit noticed the short interest.
Someone did the maths out loud.

**REDDIT:** "Wait. If they bet against more shares than exist..."
**REDDIT:** "...and we all just buy and hold..."
**REDDIT:** "...they have to buy from *us*."

The second week of January, the stock was under twenty dollars.
By Friday the twenty-second, sixty-five.
By Tuesday the twenty-sixth, almost a hundred and fifty.
[DRAW: A staircase of price tags climbing up the board.]

That night, the richest man in the world posted one word.

**ELON MUSK:** "Gamestonk!!"

By Wednesday, the stock closed near three hundred and fifty dollars.

Keith Gill's fifty-three-thousand-dollar bet was now worth about forty-eight million.
[DRAW: The pickup truck from Chapter 2... now a mountain of trucks.]

**REDDIT:** "Still holding?"
**KEITH GILL:** "I like the stock."

## Chapter 5: Melvin Bleeds

For Melvin Capital, every dollar up was a dollar lost.

On Monday the twenty-fifth, two other giant firms, Citadel and Point72, put in two point seven five billion dollars to keep Melvin standing.
That's like your neighbours handing you a bucket of cash while your house is on fire.
[DRAW: A burning house. Two neighbours passing buckets labelled "$".]

By Wednesday, Melvin said it had closed out its GameStop bet.
In January alone, the fund lost more than half its money.

**MELVIN:** "Easy money."
**SAM:** "I'd like my sneakers back."

But the squeeze wasn't over. WallStreetBets grew from about two million members to more than eight million in a matter of days.
[DRAW: A tiny group chat bubble that inflates until it fills the board.]

And the internet wasn't just buying GameStop. AMC cinemas. BlackBerry. Nokia. Anything Wall Street had bet against.

**REDDIT:** "We like the stock."

And then, on Thursday morning, the most popular app in the story pressed a button.

## Chapter 6: Thursday

Thursday, the twenty-eighth of January.
[DRAW: A clock face, top-left corner. Keep it there the whole chapter.]

Before the market opens, the stock is already climbing.
In early trading, GameStop touches four hundred and eighty-three dollars a share.

Then Robinhood, the free trading app millions of young investors use, changes one thing.
You can sell GameStop.
You can't buy it.

**USER:** "Where's the buy button?"
**ROBINHOOD:** "Position closing only."
**USER:** "What does that mean?"
**ROBINHOOD:** "It means you can leave."
[DRAW: A phone with a big red BUY button, chained shut.]

Other brokers restrict trading too.
And with buyers locked out, the price falls.
By the closing bell, GameStop is down from four eighty-three to around one ninety-four.

The internet explodes.

**USER:** "They're protecting the hedge funds!"
**USER:** "Rigged!"
**USER:** "I'm deleting this app."

Politicians from both parties, who agree on almost nothing, agree on this.

**POLITICIAN 1:** "Unacceptable."
**POLITICIAN 2:** "Totally unacceptable."

So why did Robinhood really hit the brakes?

Remember "Hold that thought"? Here's the plumbing.
When you buy a stock, it takes time for the trade to fully settle. While it does, a clearinghouse sits in the middle, making sure both sides pay up.
[DRAW: Buyer and seller on either side of a bridge. A toll booth in the middle labelled "Clearinghouse".]

When trading gets wild, the clearinghouse asks brokers for a bigger safety deposit.
That Thursday morning, it asked Robinhood for about three billion dollars.

**CLEARINGHOUSE:** "Deposit, please."
**ROBINHOOD:** "How much?"
**CLEARINGHOUSE:** "About three billion."
**ROBINHOOD:** "We'll... get back to you."

That's like a corner shop being told to put down a security deposit bigger than all the cash in its tills, before lunch.
[DRAW: A tiny shopkeeper staring up at a giant "$3,000,000,000" bill.]

Restricting buying cut the risk, and the deposit came down. Over the next few days, Robinhood raised about three point four billion dollars from investors to keep going.

Not a conspiracy. Not a rescue for Wall Street either.
A small company that grew huge, and whose plumbing couldn't handle the flood.

But for millions of people watching their stock fall with no buy button, that explanation came too late.

## Chapter 7: Who Got Hurt

It's easy to think this was little guys against hedge funds, and the little guys won.
[DRAW: David and Goliath, but David is holding a phone.]

Some did win. Big.

But one week after Thursday, GameStop was trading around fifty dollars.

If you bought a thousand dollars' worth at Wednesday's close, a week later you had about a hundred and fifty.
[DRAW: A $1,000 bill being cut down to a $150 strip with scissors.]

A lot of the people who bought last were exactly the people the story said were winning. New investors. First accounts. Money they'd saved during the pandemic.

**NEW INVESTOR:** "Everyone said hold."
**NEW INVESTOR:** "I held."
**NEW INVESTOR:** "...Now what?"

And Melvin? It never really recovered. In twenty twenty-two, it shut down.

So who do you get angry at?

## Chapter 8: The Hearing

On the eighteenth of February, Congress held a hearing.
On one side of the screen: the boss of Robinhood, the boss of Melvin, the boss of Citadel, and the boss of Reddit.
And Keith Gill. From his basement. With the kitten poster behind him.
[DRAW: A grid of video-call boxes. Four suits. One headband.]

**LAWMAKER:** "Mr. Gill, why did you buy?"
**KEITH GILL:** "I like the stock."

He also cleared up an important point:

**KEITH GILL:** "I am not a cat."

He said he wasn't part of any plan. He'd made a bet, shared his reasons, and held on.

And here's the strange part. Every one of these choices made sense on its own.
**MELVIN:** "I bet on a shrinking business."
**KEITH GILL:** "I bet it was cheaper than it looked."
**REDDIT:** "We bought what we liked."
**ROBINHOOD:** "We paid the deposit we were asked for."

They just all landed in the same week.

It isn't new, either.
In twenty oh eight, investors had bet heavily against the carmaker Volkswagen. Then Porsche revealed it controlled most of the shares. The short sellers scrambled to buy what little was left.
For a moment, Volkswagen was briefly the most valuable company in the world.
[DRAW: A small car on top of a podium, wearing a crown, looking surprised.]

Then the price fell back. Squeezes are loud and fast. And they usually end.

## Chapter 9: What It Means for You

So what can ordinary investors take from all this?
[DRAW: A notepad with three numbered lines.]

**One: price isn't value.**
A stock can go up seventeen hundred percent while the business behind it barely changes. The price tells you what people will pay today. Not what the company is worth.

**Two: the app isn't the market.**
Your buy button sits on top of plumbing you can't see. On the wildest day, it can be switched off.

**Three: only put in what you could lose.**
The people who got hurt weren't the ones who were wrong about GameStop. They were the ones who couldn't afford to be.

And GameStop? It's still around. Still in the mall. Its stock still jumps whenever its biggest fan comes back online.
In twenty twenty-four, Keith Gill posted again after almost three years of silence. One picture. And the stock jumped.

Go back to that January.
A struggling shop. A fifty-three-thousand-dollar bet. A group chat that became an army.
[DRAW: Reuse the Chapter 1 storefront. The rocket is gone; the lights are still on.]

Billions of dollars moved. A hedge fund fell. An app froze. Congress asked questions.

And when they asked the man who started it why he did it, he didn't give them a theory.

**KEITH GILL:** "I like the stock."

Would you have sold at four hundred and eighty-three? Or held? Tell me in the comments.
