[VISUAL: CARD — OCT 30, 2014 · 6:07 PM · NEAR TANNERSVILLE, PA]

Thursday, October 30th, 2014. A little after six in the evening, at a defunct airfield near Tannersville, in the Pocono Mountains of Pennsylvania.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — dusk, an abandoned hangar at the edge of pine woods, cracked runway, weeds]

A team of U.S. Marshals is working its way across the property. The State Police asked them to clear this place, an abandoned resort with an old airstrip, and they've been at it for about two hours.

Their commander, a marshal named Scott Malkowski, is walking toward the hangar with two members of his team when something moves at the edge of the woods. A man is coming out of the trees, heading for the hangar. Later, telling reporters about it, Malkowski kept coming back to one detail.

"I saw the black hat."

[VISUAL: RECREATE — tiny figure in a black hat crossing the field toward the hangar; the hat stays black, everything else gray]

The marshals order him to the ground. He doesn't run, and he doesn't fight. He isn't carrying a gun. They ask him his name, and he gives them the real one.

Eric Frein.

For 48 days, he'd been the most wanted man in Pennsylvania. More than a thousand officers took part in the search at one point or another. There were roadblocks and closed schools, and a whole corner of the state was living under it. And it ends in a field next to an empty hangar, with a man in a black hat on the ground, saying his own name.

But that isn't the part people in Pennsylvania still bring up. The part they remember happened after the marshals handed him over. We'll get there.

First, the question underneath all of this. How did one man stay out of reach for seven weeks while one of the biggest searches in the state's history went on around him? Part of the answer is that police believed he'd been preparing for this, as they put it at the time, for months or maybe years.

Everything here comes from the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's opinion in his case, trial testimony, State Police and U.S. Marshals statements, and reporting from the time. Where accounts disagree, I'll tell you.

## The Barracks

[VISUAL: CARD — SEPT 12, 2014 · SHORTLY BEFORE 11 PM · BLOOMING GROVE, PA]

Seven weeks earlier. Friday night, September 12th, 2014.

The Pennsylvania State Police barracks in Blooming Grove, in Pike County, has forest pressing in on almost every side. It's shortly before eleven, and it's shift change. One group of troopers is finishing up, and another is arriving.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — the barracks at night, parking lot under one pole light, dark tree line beyond]

Corporal Bryon Dickson is one of the troopers on his way out. He's 38, from Dunmore, a Marine veteran, married with two young sons. Early accounts say he was heading toward his patrol car.

Dickson had his own assigned cruiser at Blooming Grove. Hold onto that. It comes back at the end of this story.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — a single State Police cruiser in the lot, faint amber glow on it]

A rifle shot comes out of the dark, and Dickson goes down outside the building.

Trooper Alex Douglass, 31, has just arrived for his shift along with three other troopers. He carries a gym bag out to his own vehicle, hears a noise, and sees Dickson on the ground. He goes toward him, and he's shot too.

Badly wounded, Douglass gets himself into the lobby. Two troopers pull him farther inside.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — the lobby door from outside, warm light spilling onto the pavement. No figures.]

Officers and paramedics worked on Bryon Dickson, and they couldn't save him.

Alex Douglass survived. He was in the hospital until the middle of October, then moved to a rehabilitation facility. By late March 2015, he said he had already been through sixteen surgeries, with another one scheduled.

Weeks later, police would have a few torn notebook pages describing that night. One line is in the court record. "Got a shot around 11 p.m. and took it."

[VISUAL: ARCHIVE — court exhibit page, or typeset quote labeled "COURT RECORD"]

That night, though, nobody knew who had written anything. Whoever fired those shots had disappeared into the trees.

There was no suspect and no name. They didn't even have a vehicle.

## The Swamp

That changed within days. On Sunday, September 14th, according to the court, a man walking his dog spotted a Jeep sitting partly underwater in a swampy area and reported it to State Police.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — Jeep nose-down in a dark pond, reeds, a small figure with a dog on the bank]

The Jeep was registered to Eric Frein's parents. Inside, police found a black rifle case, two spent cartridge casings, and Eric Frein's driver's license.

[VISUAL: ARCHIVE — FBI wanted poster]

His own notes would later explain the Jeep. After the shooting he'd tried to get away in it and ended up in the swamp. He called it a "disaster." Then he went into the woods on foot.

So within a couple of days, police had a name, a face and an address. What they didn't have was Eric Frein.

## Who They Were Looking For

[VISUAL: MAP — Canadensis pin and Blooming Grove barracks pin]

Eric Frein was 31 and lived with his parents in Canadensis, a small town in Barrett Township, in the same part of the Poconos.

He belonged to a military reenactment group, the kind of hobby where people put on uniforms and act out history. Frein's group portrayed Eastern European soldiers. During the manhunt, Lieutenant Colonel George Bivens of the State Police said Frein appeared to have "assumed that role in real life."

[VISUAL: RECREATE — faceless figure in a Cold War–style uniform standing alone in a gray forest]

Bivens also told reporters that Frein had talked about wanting to commit mass murder. They weren't describing a man who'd snapped one night.

Police said Frein had been preparing for months, maybe years. His computer showed research on avoiding police manhunts, on law enforcement technology, and on survival. In his recorded statement after the arrest, he said he'd used Google Earth to look at State Police barracks near his home, and that he chose Blooming Grove because of the woods around it.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — satellite-style overhead of the barracks, tree line highlighted in amber]

Hold onto that recorded statement, by the way. Years later, Pennsylvania's highest court would say the jury should never have seen it.

---

## The Search

The search that followed was enormous. Over its seven weeks, more than a thousand officers from State Police, the FBI, the U.S. Marshals and departments across the region took part. Barrett Township's own police department had six officers. Its chief said the operation was more than his department could have imagined.

[VISUAL: CLIP — short news footage of roadblocks and officers on a rural road — set up, play, break down]

At one stage, police said their focus was an area of roughly five square miles of steep, wooded ground. Later, a neighborhood called Snow Hill Falls got a lot of attention, though police said they were searching other places too.

[VISUAL: MAP — search areas highlighted one at a time with their dates, not one shrinking box]

For people who lived there, normal life stopped. Roads were blocked. Schools closed at times. Local businesses lost money as visitors stayed away. And in Barrett Township, door-to-door trick-or-treating was canceled for Halloween.

Remember that. It comes back too.

And at one point, State Police said searchers had seen a man they believed was Frein, somewhere between 75 and 100 yards away, through thick woods. They couldn't reach him.

## What He Left Behind

Here's something to keep in mind through this next part. When they finally caught him, there was a notebook at that hangar. It's the closest thing we have to hearing what those 48 days were like from his side. We'll get to it.

[VISUAL: CARD — SEPT 29, 2014]

On September 29th, searchers found a campsite. There was clothing, ammunition, Frein's checkbook, and two explosive devices. Bivens described them as fully functional pipe bombs, and he said he believed Frein had left them behind because he was under pressure.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — abandoned camp under a hemlock, scattered gear, a torn trash bag nearby]

The next day, an agent collected a trash bag near the site. Inside were three torn notebook pages describing the attack. That's where the line about taking the shot came from.

On October 8th, with Frein still out there, Bivens stood in front of reporters and read from those pages out loud.

[VISUAL: CLIP — a few seconds of the Oct 8 press briefing — set up, play, break down]

Then the search went on for three more weeks.

By the end, State Police put their own cost at about eleven million dollars. That doesn't count what the FBI and the Marshals spent.

## The Hangar

October 30th. Day 48.

So why were the marshals at that airfield at all? They weren't following a tip. State Police had asked them to clear the abandoned resort, the old Birchwood-Pocono Airpark near Tannersville, and they were working through it.

[VISUAL: MAP — Blooming Grove barracks pin, Birchwood-Pocono Airpark pin. Place names only.]

Malkowski said afterward, "We just had a hunch that if we were on the run, this is a place we would hide."

[VISUAL: CLIP — Malkowski interview, a few seconds — set up, play, break down]

You know what happened next. The black hat, the order to get down, his name.

There's one thing the accounts don't agree on. An early public account said nobody was hurt during the capture. But Frein's face was scraped, and the marshals themselves said that happened while he was held face down on the pavement. Both versions are on the record, so you're getting both.

He wasn't armed in that field. There were weapons inside the hangar, and so was the notebook.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — inside the hangar, a sleeping area, a rifle case, a notebook glowing faint amber]

This was the longer journal, not the three pages from the trash bag. And it doesn't read like a soldier on a mission.

On September 25th, he wrote that he "slept like a human first time in two weeks."

On October 11th, he wrote, "30 days. A lot longer than I expected to last."

[VISUAL: ARCHIVE — court exhibit, or typeset quote labeled "COURT RECORD," highlighter on "A lot longer than I expected to last"]

Police also recovered a file on a storage drive. It was a letter addressed "Mom and Dad," and in it he wrote that "the time seems right for a spark to ignite a fire in the hearts of men." The file had been created on December 29th, 2013, and last accessed on October 6th, 2014. Those dates don't tell us when any particular line was written, only when the file was made and when it was last opened.

[VISUAL: ARCHIVE — "LAST ACCESSED: OCT 6, 2014" on screen, highlighter sweep on "a spark to ignite a fire"]

That night, investigators questioned him on video. He called the killing an assassination, and the phrase that came out of that interview was that he'd wanted to "wake people up."

## The Handoff

Now, the part I told you about at the start.

The U.S. Marshals had made the arrest. But this was a State Police case, and the man they'd caught had killed one of their own. So the Marshals handed Eric Frein over to the Pennsylvania State Police.

And the troopers who took him put Corporal Bryon Dickson's handcuffs on him.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — dusk, a state trooper beside a cruiser, close on wrists behind a back, the handcuffs bright amber, everything else gray]

Then they put him in Dickson's patrol car, and they drove him back to Blooming Grove. Back to the barracks where Dickson was shot.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — night, the cruiser with faint amber glow turning into the barracks lot, same angle as the earlier barracks shot. Music out.]

State Police Commissioner Frank Noonan confirmed it. Pennsylvania's governor at the time, Tom Corbett, called it "very appropriate."

I don't think it needs anything added.

The next day was Halloween. After the arrest, Barrett Township officials told families that trick-or-treating could go ahead after all.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — a small-town street at dusk, porch lights coming on one by one in amber]

## The Verdict

The trial came in April 2017, in Pike County. Alex Douglass testified. On April 19th, the jury convicted Eric Frein on all twelve counts, including first-degree murder of a law enforcement officer and terrorism.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — empty courtroom diorama, a single amber light over the witness stand]

Then came the penalty phase, to decide whether he'd live. On April 20th, Bryon Dickson's widow, Tiffany Dickson, testified about raising their two sons without their father. Frein's parents testified too, asking for his life.

On April 26th, the jury returned a death verdict. They found no mitigating circumstances. The judge formally imposed the sentence the next day.

## The Confession

Remember that recorded statement from the night of the arrest?

On appeal, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court ruled that the trial judge should have kept it out. Police had kept questioning Frein after he had repeatedly said he wanted to remain silent.

[VISUAL: ARCHIVE — Pa. Supreme Court opinion, highlighter sweep on the ruling]

But the court still upheld his conviction and his death sentence. The justices found the rest of the evidence so overwhelming that the error couldn't have affected the verdict. That evidence included the Jeep, the casings, the campsite, the pages in the trash bag, and the journal.

In January 2020, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to take the case. His lawyers have kept litigating since then, in post-conviction proceedings.

He is on Pennsylvania's death row. Pennsylvania hasn't carried out an execution since 1999.

## What It Actually Was

Here's what stays with me about this one.

Eric Frein put a lot of work into a character. There was the Cold War soldier in the woods, the research on manhunts, the barracks studied on Google Earth, and the letter about a spark lighting a fire in the hearts of men.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — the reenactor figure from earlier, now small in a huge gray forest]

Then there's his own notebook. "Slept like a human first time in two weeks." "A lot longer than I expected to last."

The revolution in that letter never came. What ended it was a team of marshals working through an abandoned property they'd been asked to clear, and a black hat coming out of the trees at dusk. He gave his real name lying in a field. The confession he gave that night turned out to be the one piece of evidence the courts said shouldn't have been used, and it didn't matter, because everything else was enough.

What people remember now isn't anything Frein wrote. They remember a pair of handcuffs, and a patrol car pulling back into the parking lot at Blooming Grove.
