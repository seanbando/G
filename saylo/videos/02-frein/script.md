[VISUAL: CARD — OCT 30, 2014 · 6:00 PM · TANNERSVILLE, PA]

Thursday, October 30th, 2014. About six in the evening. A closed airport in the Pocono Mountains of Pennsylvania.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — dusk, an abandoned hangar at the edge of pine woods, cracked runway, weeds]

A team of U.S. Marshals is walking the property. They aren't responding to a tip, and nobody has called in a sighting. They're checking it because it looks like the kind of place a man on the run would sleep.

Then their commander, a marshal named Scott Malkowski, sees something move at the tree line. A figure is walking out of the woods toward the old hangar. Later, describing it to reporters, he kept coming back to one detail.

"I saw the black hat."

[VISUAL: RECREATE — tiny figure in a black hat crossing a field toward the hangar, the hat the only amber thing in frame]

The marshals close in and order him to the ground. He doesn't run, and he doesn't fight. He isn't even carrying a gun. When they ask his name, he gives the real one.

Eric Frein.

For 48 days, more than a thousand officers had been looking for this man. Roadblocks, closed schools, an entire corner of Pennsylvania told to stay indoors. And it ends in a field next to an empty hangar, with a guy in a black hat lying on the ground saying his own name.

But that isn't the part people in Pennsylvania still talk about. The part they remember happened about an hour later, on the drive back. We'll get there.

First, the question that matters. How did one man stay hidden for seven weeks from one of the biggest manhunts in the state's history? The short version is that the police were hunting a man who had spent years planning to be hunted.

Everything here comes from court testimony, State Police and U.S. Marshals statements, and reporting from the time. Where accounts disagree, I'll tell you.

## The Barracks

[VISUAL: CARD — SEPT 12, 2014 · 10:50 PM · BLOOMING GROVE, PA]

Seven weeks earlier. Friday night, September 12th, 2014.

The Pennsylvania State Police barracks in Blooming Grove sits in Pike County with forest pressing in on almost every side. It's a little before eleven, and it's shift change. One group of troopers is going home, and another is coming in.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — the barracks at night, parking lot lit by one pole light, dark tree line across the road]

Corporal Bryon Dickson is one of the troopers heading out. He's 38, from Dunmore, a Marine veteran, married with two young sons. He walks out of the building toward his patrol car.

Hold onto that car. It comes back at the end of this story.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — a single State Police cruiser in the lot, faint amber glow on it]

He doesn't reach it. A rifle shot comes out of the darkness and hits him. He goes down near the entrance.

A few moments later, Trooper Alex Douglass pulls into the lot to start his shift. He's 31. He sees Dickson on the ground, gets out, and is shot too. According to accounts of that night, Douglass, badly wounded, managed to crawl into the lobby, where another trooper pulled him into a secure part of the building.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — the lobby door from outside, warm light spilling onto the pavement. No figures.]

Bryon Dickson died at the scene. Alex Douglass survived. He stayed in the hospital until the middle of October, then went to a rehabilitation facility, and six months after the shooting he was getting ready for his sixteenth surgery.

Months later, investigators would read a line in the shooter's own handwriting about that night. "Got a shot around 11 p.m. and took it."

[VISUAL: ARCHIVE — crumpled notebook page, highlighter sweep on the line]

That night, though, nobody knew who had written anything. Troopers locked down the building, and police flooded the area. Whoever fired those shots had disappeared into the trees across the road.

There was no suspect and no name. At that moment they didn't even have a vehicle.

## The Swamp

About three days later, a man walking his dog near a swampy retention pond, roughly two miles from the barracks, notices something that doesn't belong. It's a Jeep, sitting half underwater.

He calls 911.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — Jeep nose-down in a dark pond, reeds, a small figure with a dog on the bank, flashlight beam in amber]

Inside it, police find two empty rifle cases, camouflage face paint, and military-style gear. They also find shell casings that match the ones at the barracks, and a Pennsylvania driver's license.

The name on it is Eric Matthew Frein.

[VISUAL: ARCHIVE — FBI wanted poster]

It came out later, from his own notes, what had happened to the Jeep. After the shooting he'd tried to drive away and ended up in the swamp. He called it a "disaster." Then he left the Jeep, with his license in it, and went into the woods on foot.

So in about three days, police went from nothing to a name, a face and an address. All they had to do now was find him.

That took forty-five more days.

## Who They Were Looking For

[VISUAL: MAP — Canadensis pin, Blooming Grove barracks pin, swamp pin, all within a short drive]

Eric Frein was 31 and lived with his parents in Canadensis, a small town in Barrett Township, not far from the barracks.

He belonged to a military reenactment group, the kind of hobby where people dress up and act out battles. But Frein's group portrayed Eastern European soldiers from the Cold War era. Police said that during the manhunt he appeared to have "assumed that role in real life."

[VISUAL: RECREATE — faceless figure in a Cold War–style uniform standing alone in a gray forest]

He'd also made several trips to Southeast Europe, according to police. And when State Police briefed reporters during the search, they said he'd talked about wanting to commit mass murder. They weren't describing a man who'd snapped one night.

The more investigators dug, the worse it looked. Police said he'd been planning this for years. His computer showed research on how to avoid police manhunts, on law enforcement technology, and on survival skills. In his confession, he said he'd used Google Earth to look at State Police barracks near his home, and he picked Blooming Grove because it was surrounded by woods. The woods gave him cover.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — satellite-style overhead of the barracks, tree line highlighted in amber]

That's the first thing to understand about why this took so long. Most fugitives are running from something they didn't plan for. Frein had spent years planning for exactly this part.

## The Box

Police believed he hadn't gone far. They drew a box around his parents' home, roughly five square miles of steep, thick forest in Pike and Monroe counties, and they started closing it.

[VISUAL: MAP — topographic box, roughly 5 square miles, search grid lines in thin white]

At its peak, the search had more than a thousand officers in it, from State Police, the FBI, the U.S. Marshals and departments across the region. Barrett Township's own police force had six officers. Its chief said the operation was more than his department could have imagined.

[VISUAL: CLIP — short news footage of roadblocks and officers on a rural road — set up, play, break down]

For the people who lived there, normal life stopped. Schools closed at times. Roads were blocked. Residents were told to stay inside. When Halloween came around, communities moved trick-or-treating indoors.

And Frein was out there, close. At one point State Police said there had been a credible sighting from somewhere between 75 and 100 yards away, through thick woods. They couldn't close the distance.

Think about that for a second. A thousand officers in a five-mile box, someone close enough to see him, and he still got away.

Officers had been authorized to use deadly force. Over time the search narrowed to a rural neighborhood called Snow Hill Falls, which backs up against state forest. Local businesses started counting their losses, because tourists stopped coming and the people who lived there stopped going out.

[VISUAL: MAP — the box shrinking toward Snow Hill Falls, state forest edge in amber]

There was one thing the searchers were counting on, and it was the calendar. In the Poconos, the leaves start dropping in October. Reporters at the time wrote about it openly. Once the canopy thinned and the nights got cold, a man in the woods would be easier to see, and a lot more miserable.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — the same forest diorama, leaves falling, branches thinning, frost on the ground]

So the plan, more or less, was to keep the pressure on and wait for the woods to stop hiding him.

## What He Left Behind

Here's something to keep in mind through this next part. The marshal who finally found him wasn't searching that box. He was looking somewhere else entirely, and he only went there because he asked himself one question. We'll get to what it was.

[VISUAL: CARD — SEPT 29, 2014]

On September 29th, searchers found a campsite. It had been left in a hurry. There was food, clothing, ammunition and explosives. They also found his checkbook and his cellphone, and inside a trash bag, a few damp, crumpled pages from a notebook.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — abandoned camp under a hemlock, a torn trash bag, notebook pages glowing faint amber]

Not long after, searchers found two pipe bombs in the woods. State Police said they were fully functional, and they had trip wires and fuses. Lieutenant Colonel George Bivens, who was leading the investigation, said he believed Frein had dropped them because he was under pressure.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — a thin wire across a forest trail, catching amber light]

That changes how a search works. Once you know there could be a trip wire across a trail, nobody walks that trail fast. Every step slows down.

The notebook pages are where you start to hear him. According to police and later trial testimony, the entries describe his days on the run one at a time, including how he was avoiding the people looking for him. Prosecutors later read his entries to a jury, and they don't sound like a soldier on a mission. They sound like someone getting tired.

One entry, from October 10th, just reads "30 days."

Another says he "slept like a human first time in two weeks."

[VISUAL: ARCHIVE — notebook page, highlighter on "30 days"]

Reporters who sat through the trial described those entries as weary, and surprised. He hadn't expected it to last this long.

Neither had anyone else. By the end, State Police put the cost of the search at about eleven million dollars, and that was only their share. The FBI and the Marshals had their own bills.

## The Hunch

October 30th. Day 48.

Remember what Malkowski was doing that evening. He wasn't following a tip, and he wasn't walking the five-mile box. His team was checking places they thought a man on the run would pick, based on one question.

If I were him, where would I hide?

The answer they came up with was a closed airport. The Birchwood-Pocono Airpark near Tannersville was closed, and it still had an abandoned hangar standing. It's about thirty miles from the barracks.

[VISUAL: MAP — barracks pin → airpark pin, dotted line, "~30 MILES" in amber]

According to local reporting, some officers who were called in at first went to the wrong airport. That's how unexpected this location was.

Malkowski told reporters afterward, "We just had a hunch that if we were on the run, this is a place we would hide."

[VISUAL: CLIP — Malkowski interview, a few seconds — set up, play, break down]

The black hat came out of the trees, and you know the rest. One thing did change after that first night's story. In the photos from the arrest, Frein's face is scraped up. Police at first said the injuries didn't happen during the capture. The marshals later said they did, while he was held face down on the pavement. I'm telling you because both versions are on the record. He wasn't armed, though there were weapons inside the hangar, including a rifle police later tested. Accounts differ on whether he was in the field or right at the hangar door when they reached him. Every account agrees he didn't fight.

That night, in a room with State Police investigators, Frein gave a videotaped confession. He called the shooting an assassination, meant to "wake people up."

That confession would matter for years. His lawyers tried to keep it away from the jury, and a judge let it in. Later, his appeal argued the questioning that night had violated his right to a lawyer. Hold onto that, because it's where his case ended up.

Investigators also found a letter on a storage drive, addressed "Mom and Dad." In it he'd written that "the time seems right for a spark to ignite a fire in the hearts of men." According to reporting on the evidence, the file was created on December 29th, 2013, nine months before the shooting, and last opened on October 6th, 2014, while he was hiding in the woods.

[VISUAL: ARCHIVE — highlighter sweep on "a spark to ignite a fire"]

## The Ride Back

Now, the part I told you about at the start.

The U.S. Marshals had caught him. But this was a State Police case, and the man they'd caught had killed one of their own. So the Marshals turned Eric Frein over to the Pennsylvania State Police.

The troopers put him in handcuffs. Specifically, they used Corporal Bryon Dickson's handcuffs.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — close on wrists behind a back, the handcuffs bright amber, everything else gray]

Then they put him in the back of Dickson's patrol car, the one he'd been walking toward when he was shot. And they drove him to the Blooming Grove barracks, the same building, the same parking lot.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — night, the cruiser with faint amber glow turning into the barracks lot, same angle as the opening barracks shot. Music out.]

State Police Commissioner Frank Noonan confirmed it to reporters. Pennsylvania's governor at the time, Tom Corbett, said he thought it was "very appropriate."

I'll leave it at that. I don't think it needs anything added.

Two weeks later, on November 12th, exactly two months after the ambush, the Blooming Grove barracks reopened to the public.

## The Verdict

The trial came in April 2017, in Pike County. Alex Douglass testified. Dickson's widow testified about raising their two sons without their father, and told the court she was angry now.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — empty courtroom diorama, a single amber light over the witness stand]

The jury convicted Frein on all twelve charges, including first-degree murder of a law enforcement officer and terrorism.

Then came the hearing on whether he'd live. His mother asked the jury to spare his life. His father, Michael Frein, testified that he had shared anti-government views with his son, but said he'd never encouraged him to go after police. Prosecutors told the jury not to read much into it. On April 26th, 2017, the same jury sentenced him to death. His lawyer said he was surprised they didn't find a single mitigating factor.

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court upheld the conviction and the sentence in 2019. In January 2020, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear his appeal, the one built around that night's confession.

He is on Pennsylvania's death row. Pennsylvania hasn't carried out an execution since 1999, so it's an open question whether that sentence will ever be carried out.

## What It Actually Was

Here's what stays with me about this one.

Eric Frein spent years building a character. A Cold War soldier in the woods, a man who researched manhunts and studied barracks on Google Earth, who wrote to his parents about a spark lighting a fire in the hearts of men. He planned the ambush and he planned the escape, and for seven weeks it worked better than almost anyone expected.

[VISUAL: RECREATE — the reenactor figure from earlier, now small in a huge gray forest]

Then his own notebook shows what was left of the plan. Thirty days. Sleeping like a human for the first time in two weeks.

The revolution he wrote about never came. What ended it was a marshal asking himself where a tired man would go to sleep, and a black hat crossing an empty field at dusk. He gave his real name on the ground in a field.

And the thing people remember now isn't anything Frein wrote. It's a pair of handcuffs, and a patrol car pulling back into the same parking lot where Bryon Dickson never made it to his car.
