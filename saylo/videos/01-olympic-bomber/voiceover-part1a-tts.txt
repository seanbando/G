Three thirty in the morning.

May thirty-first, two thousand three.

Murphy, North Carolina — a mountain town of about sixteen hundred people where, at that hour, the only thing moving is the occasional patrol car.

The officer in that car is twenty-one years old. His name is Jeff Postell, and he's been on the job for less than a year. He swings around behind the Save-A-Lot grocery store with his lights off, and he spots a man crouched by the dumpsters.

Postell figures it's a burglar. The man bolts, then gives up. Thin, clean-shaven, polite. He says his name is Jerry Wilson. Homeless. Just passing through from Ohio.

It's a lie, and a pretty good one.

For five years, the man standing in that parking lot had been on the FBI's Ten Most Wanted list with a million dollars on his head. Helicopters with heat cameras had flown over these exact hills looking for him. At one point more than two hundred federal agents were working out of this corner of the state.

His real name was Eric Rudolph.

And seven years earlier, after a bomb went off at the Atlanta Olympics, the FBI had spent eighty-eight days chasing someone else.

So why was he so hard to catch? There isn't one answer. There are three, and they stack on top of each other. For the first eighty-eight days, the FBI was looking at the wrong man — the guard who'd helped save people that night, which is the part I honestly find hardest to read about. Then for a year and a half, the real bomber didn't have a name. And once he finally did, he walked into a place that was built for hiding.

I'm working from court records, the FBI's own case history, a Justice Department review, and news reporting from the time. When the record is fuzzy, I'll say so.

Saturday, July twenty-seventh, nineteen ninety-six. The Olympics are in Atlanta, and if you didn't have tickets to anything, Centennial Olympic Park was where you went. It was free. A little after midnight there were thousands of people packed in front of a stage, and a band called Jack Mack and the Heart Attack was playing.

Working security near the big sound and light tower is a thirty-three-year-old named Richard Jewell. Former sheriff's deputy. Now he's a contract guard for A-T-and-T, which is not where he wanted his career to be, but it's a job.

Shortly before one in the morning, Jewell notices a green military-style backpack under a bench near the tower. Nobody's sitting there and nobody comes back for it.

Jewell points it out to Georgia Bureau of Investigation officers. One of them calls the bomb squad, and Jewell starts helping push people back. Which is harder than it sounds — you try telling a crowd at one in the morning to leave a concert because of a bag.

At twelve fifty-eight, a call comes into nine-one-one from a payphone a few blocks away. A man, very calm, says there's a bomb in Centennial Park and you've got thirty minutes. According to later reporting the dispatcher had trouble even routing the warning, because the park's address wasn't in her system. The park was brand new.

He said thirty minutes.

It went off at about one twenty.

There's a detail hiding in that phone call, by the way. Months from now, it'll matter more than anything else about Richard Jewell. We'll get there.

Three pipe bombs, packed with nails. Alice Hawthorne, forty-four, from Albany, Georgia, was killed — she'd come down with her fourteen-year-old daughter, Fallon, who was hurt in the blast. Over a hundred other people were injured. A Turkish TV cameraman named Melih Uzunyol had a heart attack running to film it, and died.

Investigators said afterward that the evacuation Jewell helped start kept the numbers from being a lot worse.

For three days, Richard Jewell was a hero. He went on the Today show. Then on July thirtieth the Atlanta Journal-Constitution put out a special edition, and the headline said the FBI suspected the "hero" guard may have planted the bomb.

It started with a tip. Before the Olympics, Jewell had worked as a campus police officer at Piedmont College in north Georgia. After the bombing, people from the college contacted investigators with concerns about how he'd behaved there.

That tip pointed the FBI toward a theory. There have been cases where someone plants a device so they can find it and be the hero. Jewell was an ex-cop who badly wanted to be a cop again, he lived with his mom, and he'd been doing interviews all week.

Suspicion isn't evidence, though. Nothing physical tied him to the backpack, and no witness put him near it before he found it.

Didn't matter.

Once his name leaked, reporters camped outside his mother's apartment, agents carried boxes of their stuff out on live TV, and Jay Leno was making jokes about him.

It wasn't only the newspaper. On NBC, Tom Brokaw reportedly told viewers the FBI probably had enough to arrest him. NBC later paid Jewell a settlement over that. CNN settled with him too.

Jewell's mother, Bobi, eventually held her own press conference. She was crying, and she asked President Clinton directly to clear her son's name. Picture that — a mother who'd watched agents carry her things out of her apartment on live TV, now begging the President of the United States on camera.

A Justice Department review later went through how agents handled him, and one detail from it still gets me. They brought Jewell in by telling him he'd be helping make a training video for first responders. Then, partway through, they presented his Miranda rights as if that were part of the video too. That's specifically what the Justice Department criticized.

And the thing that should've cleared him was sitting there the whole time.

It's in the phone records. A G-B-I agent called the bomb squad at twelve fifty-seven, right after Jewell pointed out the bag. One minute later, at twelve fifty-eight, the bomber called nine-one-one from a payphone several blocks away.

One minute.

Jewell's lawyers kept coming back to that, and they were right to. For Jewell to be the caller, he'd have had to point out the bag and then get to a payphone several blocks away, through a packed park, in about sixty seconds.

The question of whether Jewell could have done it didn't need a profile. It needed a clock and a map.

On October twenty-sixth, nineteen ninety-six — eighty-eight days in — the U-S Attorney's office sent his lawyers a letter saying he was no longer a target. Attorney General Janet Reno later said publicly she was sorry about the leak.

That's three months with the investigation's attention on the wrong man. That's the first reason he was so hard to catch.

And the real bomber wasn't finished.
