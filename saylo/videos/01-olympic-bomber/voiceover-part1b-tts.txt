January sixteenth, nineteen ninety-seven. A bomb goes off at a building in Sandy Springs, north of Atlanta, where an abortion clinic has its offices. Police show up. Firefighters, federal agents. About an hour later, while they're working the scene, a second bomb explodes in the parking lot.

That second one was for them.

It was set to hit whoever came running, and several responders were hurt.

A month after that, February twenty-first, it's the Otherside Lounge, a lesbian bar in Atlanta. Five people injured. Police sweep the area and find a second device outside — the same trick — and the bomb squad takes care of it before it goes off.

Letters start showing up at news organizations, signed "Army of God," taking credit.

By now investigators are pretty sure it's one person. Same construction, same habits, same kind of target. What they don't have is a name, and they won't get one for almost another year.

January twenty-ninth, nineteen ninety-eight. About seven thirty in the morning, outside the New Woman All Women Health Care clinic in Birmingham, Alabama.

Robert Sanderson is a Birmingham police officer working an off-duty security shift there. According to accounts of that morning, he'd noticed something by the entrance and gone over to check it. He's killed. A nurse, Emily Lyons, is torn up by the blast — she lost an eye and went through years of surgeries. This bomb was set off by remote control, which means whoever did it was close enough to watch.

And then something happens that hadn't happened at any of the other scenes.

When a bomb goes off, people run toward it, or they freeze. A college student nearby noticed one guy doing neither. He was walking away from it. Not fast. Just strolling off like he'd finished an errand.

That bugged the student enough to follow him. Another witness got involved, saw the man climb into a gray Nissan pickup, and wrote down the license plate.

I want to point that out, because it's the whole story in miniature. Two years of profiles, task forces and FBI theories, and the case breaks because somebody noticed a man walking the wrong way and grabbed a pen.

The truck comes back to a thirty-one-year-old from western North Carolina. Eric Robert Rudolph.

A year and a half after Atlanta, the bomber finally has a name. That's reason number two — for most of that time, nobody could put one on him.

Rudolph was born in nineteen sixty-six in Merritt Island, Florida. His dad died in nineteen eighty-one, and his mother moved the kids up to the mountains around Topton and Murphy, right on the edge of the Nantahala National Forest.

According to reporting from the time, the family spent part of the nineteen eighties around a church in Missouri tied to Christian Identity, which is a white supremacist religious movement. Rudolph went into the Army in nineteen eighty-seven, served with the hundred and first Airborne at Fort Campbell, Kentucky, and was out by nineteen eighty-nine.

Then he went home. Back to the woods.

People who knew him said the same thing over and over — he knew that forest. Hunting, camping, the old logging roads, the hollows. About a week after Birmingham, agents found his gray Nissan abandoned near Murphy. Hold onto the name of that town. It comes back at the very end of this story.

And he was just gone. Into more than half a million acres of mountain that he knew better than anyone looking for him.
